The eml(x, y) Function — Definition, Properties & Significance 🧮

Definition

$$\text{eml}(x, y) = e^x - \ln(y)$$

Two inputs. One operator. Deceptively simple — and, as it turns out, extraordinarily powerful.

$$\text{eml} : \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}^+ \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$$

Variable Constraint Reason
$x$ $(-\infty, +\infty)$ $e^x$ is defined everywhere
$y$ $(0, +\infty)$ $\ln(y)$ requires $y > 0$
Output $(-\infty, +\infty)$ Full real range

Mathematical Properties

Partial Derivatives

$$\frac{\partial \text{eml}}{\partial x} = e^x \qquad \frac{\partial \text{eml}}{\partial y} = -\frac{1}{y}$$

  • Always strictly positive in $x$ → monotonically increasing
  • Always strictly negative in $y$ → monotonically decreasing

Hessian Matrix

$$H = \begin{pmatrix} e^x & 0 \\ 0 & \frac{1}{y^2} \end{pmatrix}$$

Both diagonal entries are strictly positive → positive definite everywhere → strictly convex in both variables simultaneously.

Gradient & Critical Points

$$\nabla \text{eml} = \left(e^x,\ -\frac{1}{y}\right)$$

Since $e^x > 0$ always and $-1/y < 0$ always, the gradient is never zero.

eml has no critical points — no local minima, maxima, or saddle points anywhere on its domain.

Mixed Partial Derivative

$$\frac{\partial^2 \text{eml}}{\partial x \, \partial y} = 0$$

The variables are completely decoupled, making eml separable:

$$\text{eml}(x, y) = g(x) + h(y) \quad \text{where } g(x) = e^x,\ h(y) = -\ln(y)$$

Zero Level Set

Setting $\text{eml}(x, y) = 0$:

$$e^x = \ln(y) \implies y = e^{e^x}$$

This is a double exponential — an extraordinarily fast-growing curve:

$x$ $y = e^{e^x}$
$0$ $e^1 \approx 2.718$
$1$ $e^e \approx 15.15$
$2$ $e^{e^2} \approx 1{,}618{,}177$

Symmetry & Scaling

$$\text{eml}(x + c,\ y) = e^c \cdot e^x - \ln(y)$$ $$\text{eml}(x,\ y \cdot k) = e^x - \ln(y) - \ln(k)$$

Shifting $x$ scales the exponential term; scaling $y$ shifts the logarithmic term — a pleasing asymmetry.

Why It's Remarkable — Universal Completeness

A paper published on arXiv (2603.21852) makes a striking claim:

A single binary operator eml(x,y), together with the constant 1, can generate every elementary function a scientific calculator can compute.

This includes:

  • ✅ All arithmetic: +, −, ×, ÷, exponentiation
  • ✅ Constants: integers, fractions, radicals, π, e
  • ✅ All transcendental functions: sin, cos, tan, ln, exp, sqrt
  • ✅ Everything else on a scientific calculator

The NAND Analogy

In digital electronics, the NAND gate is universal — any Boolean circuit can be built from NAND gates alone. Continuous mathematics has never had an equivalent... until now.

eml is to continuous mathematics what NAND is to Boolean logic.

Every EML expression is a binary tree of identical nodes — a beautifully uniform structure that is also well-suited to gradient-based symbolic regression (training AI to discover mathematical laws from data).

The author found this by exhaustive systematic search, and notes this may be just the tip of the iceberg. Preliminary work has already found related operators with even stronger properties, including a ternary variant requiring no distinguished constant at all.

Connections to Other Fields

Because eml splits cleanly into independent components, it appears naturally in:

  • Maximum likelihood estimation — log-likelihoods involve $\ln(y)$
  • Information theory — $-\ln(p)$ is the self-information of probability $p$
  • Thermodynamics — $e^x$ terms arise in Boltzmann distributions
